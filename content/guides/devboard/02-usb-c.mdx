title: USB-C
---

We have our power decoupling, but we don't actually have a power source yet
or a way to program our devboard yet, so let's do that now. I'm going to be
using USB-C because it's standard and fast!

<Step title="Add the USB-C receptacle">
- Tap **"a"**, type in whatever receptacle you want, and add it in. Make
  sure you pick **"receptacle"** and not plug because a plug would plug
  into your laptop instead of having a cable plug into it.

  ![USB-C receptacle symbol](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/KaiPereira/build-a-devboard/master/journal/Pasted%20image%2020250925103733.png)
</Step>

Now let's explain each of these pins:
- **SHIELD/GND** will both go to ground, shield is conductive material
  wrapped around the data pins on the receptacle, and this just improves
  EMI by grounding it.
- **D+/D-** are the data pins, these transfer data to/from the USB-C
  receptacle. You'll want to connect the D-'s and D+'s together so that
  they both transfer data.
- **CC1 and CC2** basically tell the receptacle to allow power to go
  through to power the board. These by standard (the datasheet tells you)
  are pulled down (go to GND) through 5.1K resistors.
- **VBUS** is the 5V input, this will need to be stepped down to 3.3V to
  power our MCU (microcontroller).

<Step title="Ground shield/GND">
- Now that we know what everything does, let's wire it up. Shield/GND go
  to GND:

  ![Shield and GND wired](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/KaiPereira/build-a-devboard/master/journal/Pasted%20image%2020250925105339.png)
</Step>

<Step title="Label the data lines">
- D+ and D- are attached to their relative pair, and then will go into the
  MCU, but for now, we'll just have a global label going out of them.
- Global labels are basically like little teleporters, that allow you to
  say that something is wiring, without manually putting a wire between
  them. Technically global labels are meant to be used between different
  schematic sheets and net labels would be the correct thing to use here,
  but I find global labels are cleaner if you only have one schematic for
  your PCB.
- Tap **global label** in the right hand toolbar, type in the name for
  your label (**USB_D+** and **USB_D-**), and add them to the pins:

  ![Global labels on the data lines](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/KaiPereira/build-a-devboard/master/journal/Pasted%20image%2020250925164006.png)
</Step>

<Step title="Pull down the CC pins">
- Pulldown the CC pins through a 5.1K resistor to GND to enable power to
  go through the USB-C receptacle. Open up the symbol library, and then
  type **"r"** the shorthand for resistors, and then place it down and
  edit the value to be 5.1K:

  ![CC pulldown resistors](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/KaiPereira/build-a-devboard/master/journal/Pasted%20image%2020250925163923.png)

- The CC pulldowns are one of the most common examples of <ConceptLink slug="pull-up-resistors">pull resistors</ConceptLink> in
  the wild - here pulling the line *down* to GND to tell the receptacle to
  allow power through.
</Step>

<Warning title="Legibility beats density">
Remember to follow proper schematic good practices, and to have clearly
visible values, and labels for your components. Feel free to edit the text
of stuff to make your schematic cleaner, just don't make stuff *too* small.
</Warning>

## The LDO

Now we just need to wire in the input voltage, but the thing is, the
voltage of USB-C is <ConceptLink slug="voltage">5V</ConceptLink>, while the
voltage that the RP2040 uses as input, needs to be 3.3V so you don't cook
it. To achieve this, we'll use what's called an LDO, or a Low-Dropout
Regulator to take the voltage down.

Specifically, we'll be using the **NCP1117**, a classic and reliable *fixed*
voltage regulator **(I actually switch this regulator out later for the
MCP1700, 3.3V, because it's really big on our PCB)**. A fixed voltage
regulator is handy here, because we only need to go down to 3.3V instead of
like 1.5V per say or something random, and it uses less components. We'll
also be using the SOT-223 footprint (or package is the common term) because
it's small and we don't really have any thermal issues with a devboard.

<Step title="Wire the LDO">
- Add in the **NCP1117-3.3_SOT223** symbol, wire GND and attach VBUS to the
  VI (voltage input) of the LDO.

  ![LDO input wired](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/KaiPereira/build-a-devboard/master/journal/Pasted%20image%2020250925163857.png)
</Step>

Remember to always keep your schematic clean and feel free to use up quite
a bit of space. Now like the decoupling capacitors on our RP2040, we need
capacitors on the LDO. But we don't need fine decoupling capacitors for
precise input lines into an MCU, and instead we need **bulk** capacitors,
to handle the large voltage ripples when moving a voltage down.

<Step title="Add the bulk caps">
- So we need to place two, 10uF capacitors on each side of the LDO, for
  input/output, so add them into your schematic:

  ![Bulk caps on the LDO](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/KaiPereira/build-a-devboard/master/journal/Pasted%20image%2020250925163830.png)
</Step>

<Step title="Label the power rails">
- Next, we want to add our power labels to the LDO, we'll put a VBUS label
  **before** the LDO/Bulk cap, and a +3V3 label to the VO (voltage out) of
  the LDO. We might use 5V to power some other devices so we'll want to
  provide a power line for that too:

  ![Power rails labelled](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/KaiPereira/build-a-devboard/master/journal/Pasted%20image%2020250925163756.png)
</Step>

## Termination resistors

Now to finish off the USB-C wiring, we need to make sure the MCU receives
the data lines. It's standard to have these going through 27 ohm resistors
into the MCU to prevent distortions of the signals at high speeds, these
are called *termination resistors*.

<Step title="Route the data pairs through the resistors">
- Wire the USB D+ and D- pairs into the MCU USB_DP and USB_DM (the P is
  for + and the M is for -) through 27 ohm resistors:

  ![Termination resistors in place](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/KaiPereira/build-a-devboard/master/journal/Pasted%20image%2020250925164239.png)
</Step>

<Step title="Mark the data labels bidirectional">
- Now USB D+ and D- are actually what's called "bidirectional", this means
  that they work both ways. You don't actually need to specify this, but
  good schematic practices is to make sure your global labels reflect
  that.
- Currently they're just set as "inputs" because the triangle is facing
  inwards, so double click on all the D+ and D- labels and set them to
  bidirectional:

  ![Bidirectional labels](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/KaiPereira/build-a-devboard/master/journal/Pasted%20image%2020250926064330.png)
</Step>
