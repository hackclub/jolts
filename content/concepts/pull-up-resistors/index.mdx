Wire a button between a pin and ground, read the pin, and you'll get…
garbage. Pressed reads low, sure - but *released* reads low, high, or
random noise depending on the weather. The pin is **floating**: connected
to nothing, it picks up whatever interference is nearby, like a radio
antenna tuned to static.

## The fix

Give the pin a default. A **pull-up resistor** connects the pin to the
positive supply through a weak resistor (usually 10 kΩ):

- Button released → nothing else drives the pin → the resistor gently
  "pulls it up" → reads **high**, reliably.
- Button pressed → the pin is connected straight to ground → ground wins
  (a direct wire beats a 10 kΩ resistor) → reads **low**, reliably.

The resistor's whole job is to lose politely. It sets the answer when
nobody's talking and steps aside the moment anything real drives the
line. That's also why the value barely matters - anything from 4.7 kΩ to
100 kΩ works for a button.

Note the logic comes out *inverted*: pressed = `False`. Every beginner
trips on this once; it's not a bug.

## You rarely add one yourself

Microcontrollers ship with pull-ups built into every pin - you enable
them in software:

```python
import board, digitalio
btn = digitalio.DigitalInOut(board.GP10)
btn.switch_to_input(pull=digitalio.Pull.UP)
```

The big exception is <ConceptLink slug="i2c" />: its two shared lines
need real, physical pull-up resistors because devices only ever pull the
lines low. Breakout boards almost always solder them on for you.
