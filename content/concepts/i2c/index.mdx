I2C ("eye-squared-see") is the protocol behind most of the little boards
you'll plug into a microcontroller - OLED displays, temperature sensors,
accelerometers. Its trick: **two shared wires**, no matter how many
devices join.

## The two wires

- **SDA** - data. Bits go both directions on this one wire.
- **SCL** - clock. The controller ticks this line so everyone agrees when
  each bit happens.

Every device hangs off the same two lines, plus power and ground. Compare
that to wiring a display with 8+ dedicated pins and you see why I2C won.

## Addresses

With everyone on one wire, messages need names. Each device has a 7-bit
**address** - the SSD1306 OLED is usually `0x3C`, for instance. The
controller starts every conversation by calling an address; only that
device answers.

This is also your first debugging tool. In CircuitPython:

```python
import board, busio
i2c = busio.I2C(board.GP1, board.GP0)  # SCL, SDA
while not i2c.try_lock():
    pass
print([hex(a) for a in i2c.scan()])
```

If your device's address prints, the wiring is right - full stop. If the
list is empty, no amount of driver code will help: check SDA/SCL aren't
swapped (the universal first mistake), then power and ground.

## Why the lines idle high

I2C devices never *drive* the lines high - they only pull them low or let
go. Something has to pull the lines back up, which is exactly the job of
<ConceptLink slug="pull-up-resistors" />. Most breakout boards include
them, which is why your breadboard wiring usually just works.
