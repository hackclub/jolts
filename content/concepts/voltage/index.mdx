Every circuit question you'll ever debug comes down to three quantities.
The classic analogy is water in a pipe, and it's classic because it works.

## The three numbers

- **Voltage (V, volts)** is pressure - how hard electrons are being
  pushed. A 9 V battery pushes harder than a 1.5 V AA. Voltage is always
  measured *between two points*; "5 V" secretly means "5 V above ground."
- **Current (I, amps)** is flow - how many electrons actually move per
  second. Your circuits mostly live in milliamps (mA). An LED sips ~20 mA;
  a phone fast-charger shoves 3 A.
- **Resistance (R, ohms)** is the narrowness of the pipe - how much the
  material fights the flow. Resistors exist purely to add a known amount
  of fight.

## Ohm's law

One equation relates all three:

```
V = I × R
```

Rearrange for whatever you're missing: `I = V / R`, `R = V / I`. This is
not one formula among many - for beginner electronics, it's essentially
the *only* formula.

Worked example: an LED drops about 2 V and wants 20 mA. On a 5 V supply,
the resistor must eat the other 3 V: `R = 3 V / 0.02 A = 150 Ω`. That's
the entire mystery of "why does my LED need a resistor" - without one,
nothing limits the current, and the LED becomes very bright very briefly.

## What kills components

Two failure modes cover almost everything:

- **Too much voltage** breaks things directly - a 3.3 V part fed 5 V can
  die instantly and silently. Check the rating before wiring power.
- **Too much current** cooks things - heat is `V × I`, and small parts
  can't shed much of it.

Measuring beats guessing on both - that's the first hour with a
<Tool slug="multimeter" />.
