A multimeter turns "I think it's connected" into "it's connected." Any
$15–40 auto-ranging meter is fine - you'll use three of its modes for
years before touching the rest.

## The three modes that matter

<Step title="Continuity - the beeping one">
- Symbol: a sound-wave / diode icon. Touch the probes together; it beeps.
- Beep = the two points are electrically connected. This one mode answers
  most debugging questions: is this joint soldered? is this wire broken?
  are these two pins bridged?
- **Only use it on unpowered circuits.**
</Step>

<Step title="DC voltage - is the power right?">
- Symbol: V with straight lines (V⎓). Black probe on ground, red probe on
  the point you care about, circuit powered on.
- Fresh AA reads ~1.6 V; a "5 V" rail should read 4.75–5.25; a Pico's 3V3
  pin reads 3.3. Anything wildly off means a wiring problem upstream.
- Voltage is measured *between two points* - the number is meaningless
  without the black probe somewhere sensible. That's
  <ConceptLink slug="voltage" /> in practice.
</Step>

<Step title="Resistance - is this resistor what it claims?">
- Symbol: Ω. Probe across a component **out of circuit** (other parts in
  parallel will lie to you).
- Handy for reading unlabeled resistors faster than decoding color bands.
</Step>

<Warning title="The one way to break it">
Never measure *current* (the A socket) across a battery or power rail - that's a direct short through the meter, and it blows the internal fuse
instantly. Until you know you need current mode, just don't move the red
probe out of the V/Ω socket.
</Warning>

<Checkpoint>
Your ritual for any new circuit: continuity-check the important
connections before power, then probe the power rails right after
switch-on. Two minutes, catches ninety percent of first-power failures.
</Checkpoint>
