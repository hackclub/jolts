Soldering is not hard. It looks hard because bad joints look like blobs
and good joints look like they grew there - but the difference is about
an hour of deliberate practice, not talent.

## Pick an iron

Skip the $8 fire-starter with no temperature control. A **temperature-
controlled** iron in the $20–60 range (Pinecil, TS101, or a station like
the Hakko FX-888D) heats fast, holds its temperature when it touches
metal, and makes everything easier. Set it to **350 °C** and leave it
there - that's right for standard leaded and most lead-free solder.

Get 0.6–0.8 mm solder with a **rosin flux core**, a brass-wool tip
cleaner, and a stand.

<Warning title="The two rules">
The iron is 350 °C and looks identical hot or cold - it goes in the stand
every single time it leaves your hand. And solder in a ventilated space:
the smoke is burning flux, and your face shouldn't be in it.
</Warning>

## The first hour

<Step title="Tin the tip">
- Melt a little solder directly onto the clean tip until it's shiny and
  silver. Do this when the iron is new, and again whenever the tip looks
  dull or brown.
- A tinned tip transfers heat; an oxidized one just sits there. Ninety
  percent of "my iron doesn't work" is an untinned tip.
</Step>

<Step title="Make your first joint">
- Touch the tip so it contacts **both** the pad and the component leg at
  once. Count one second.
- Feed solder into the **joint**, not the iron. It should flow like water
  finding a drain.
- Pull the solder away, then the iron. Total contact: two to three
  seconds.
</Step>

<Step title="Read the joint">
- Good: a shiny cone, like a tiny volcano hugging the leg.
- Cold joint (dull, lumpy): the pad never got hot - reheat, hold longer.
- Ball / bridge (solder touching a neighbour): too much solder - reheat
  and drag it apart, or remove some with desoldering braid.
</Step>

<Checkpoint>
Practice on scrap perfboard with resistor legs until five joints in a
row come out cone-shaped. That's the entire skill - every project after
this is just more of the same joint.
</Checkpoint>

## Why joints fail

Solder only bonds to metal it has *wetted*, and it only wets metal that's
both hot and clean. Flux - the core of your solder wire - burns off oxide
so the wetting can happen. This is why you always melt fresh solder at
the joint instead of carrying a blob over on the tip: the blob's flux
already burned away in transit.
